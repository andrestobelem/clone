# Mission, Vision, and Values: Origin and Use

Research date: 2026-10-31

## Question

Where did the mission, vision, and values framework come from, and how should Clone use it?

## Conclusion

No single author invented the mission, vision, and values framework. The framework combines ideas that developed through different branches of management theory:

- Mission came from work on organizational purpose and strategic management.
- Vision came from work on leadership, direction, and an intended future.
- Values came from work on organizational culture and shared principles.

These ideas became a common three-part planning framework during the late twentieth century. Collins and Porras provided one of the most influential integrations in 1994 and 1996. Their model did not use the current three-file structure. It defined a vision as a core ideology, made from core purpose and core values, combined with an envisioned future.

Clone can use separate mission, vision, and values documents if each document has one clear function. The documents must guide decisions and must not repeat slogans.

## Historical development

### Mission: organizational purpose

Peter Drucker's management work made organizational purpose and mission central strategic questions. His 1973 book *Management* includes business purpose and mission as a management responsibility. Strategic-management literature then treated a mission as an enduring statement of the business's distinct purpose and scope.

John Pearce's 1981 strategic-management model defined a business mission as the fundamental purpose that distinguishes the business and identifies its operating scope. This shows that mission had become an established strategic-management concept by the early 1980s.

Mission-statement research expanded after that period. A 2011 meta-analysis reviewed 20 years of empirical research and found only a small positive relationship between mission statements and financial performance. Results varied with the measures used. A written statement alone is therefore weak evidence of organizational effectiveness.

### Values: culture and coordination

Shared values became prominent in organization design through the McKinsey 7-S framework. Tom Peters, Robert Waterman, Julien Phillips, Anthony Athos, and Richard Pascale developed the framework in the late 1970s. A 1980 article presented the model publicly, and later books made it widely known.

The framework placed shared values at the center of strategy, structure, systems, style, staff, and skills. Its main contribution was not a corporate values page. It treated values as a coordinating part of the organization that must agree with its other operating elements.

### Vision: continuity and intended future

Vision became an important leadership and strategy term during the 1980s and 1990s. Jim Collins and Jerry Porras produced an influential synthesis in *Built to Last* in 1994 and in their 1996 article “Building Your Company's Vision.”

Their framework has two parts:

1. Core ideology: core purpose and core values that remain stable.
2. Envisioned future: a long-term goal and a clear description of the intended result.

This structure separates what the organization preserves from what it intends to change or achieve. It also shows that purpose, values, and future direction are related but distinct.

### The combined framework

Mission, vision, and values became a standard planning shorthand through management education, consulting, strategic planning, and organizational development. The combination is an accumulation of the earlier ideas, not a named model with one documented inventor.

Recent management texts now present the three elements together:

- Mission explains the organization's present purpose or business.
- Vision describes its intended future.
- Values define the principles that guide conduct and choices.

The literature still contains conflicting definitions. Some authors use mission for purpose. Others include products, customers, markets, values, or ambition in the mission. Some use vision as an umbrella for purpose, values, and future direction. The labels are less important than stable definitions within one organization.

## Evidence and limits

Mission, vision, and values documents can provide common language and decision criteria. They do not create alignment by themselves.

Research and practitioner sources identify several limits:

- Mission-statement performance studies show small and inconsistent effects.
- Organizations can have clear direction without formal statements.
- A statement has little value when operations, incentives, and decisions contradict it.
- Broad or interchangeable wording does not distinguish an organization.
- Repeated content across mission, vision, and values creates maintenance cost without adding guidance.

The useful unit is therefore not the statement alone. It is the connection between the statement and repeated decisions.

## Application to Clone

Clone's documents use the following definitions:

| Document | Function | Time orientation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `MISSION.md` | State why Clone exists and what it does now. | Present |
| `VISION.md` | Describe the future that Clone intends to help create. | Future |
| `VALUES.md` | Define the standards used for choices and conduct. | Persistent |
| `CONSTITUTION.md` | Convert the purpose and values into binding product and engineering rules. | Persistent until amended |

This arrangement adapts the management framework to a software repository. The constitution is not part of the historical mission, vision, and values framework. It adds an enforcement layer: decision tests, precedence, and amendment rules.

The current Clone documents also resemble the Collins-Porras model:

| Collins-Porras concept | Clone document |
| --- | --- |
| Core purpose | `MISSION.md` |
| Core values | `VALUES.md` |
| Envisioned future | `VISION.md` |
| Governance and operating constraints | `CONSTITUTION.md` |

## Recommendations

1. Keep one function per document.
2. Use the constitution for binding rules and conflict resolution.
3. Use mission, vision, and values as context for product choices, not as release requirements.
4. Review the documents when product scope or architecture changes.
5. Test each value against actual product behavior. Remove a value that does not affect a decision.
6. Avoid claims that the framework has one inventor or one canonical definition.

### Primary and foundational sources

- Peter F. Drucker, *Management: Tasks, Responsibilities, Practices* (1973). The publisher's description lists business purpose and mission among the book's management topics. [Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/Management/Drucker/p/book/9781138129467)
- John A. Pearce II, “An Executive-Level Perspective on the Strategic Management Process” (1981). The article defines mission as a business's distinct and enduring purpose. [California Management Review](https://cmr.berkeley.edu/1981/11/24-1-an-executive-level-perspective-on-the-strategic-management-process/)
- Tom Peters, “A Brief History of the 7-S (‘McKinsey 7-S’) Model.” Peters describes the model's development and the move from “superordinate goals” to “shared values.” [Tom Peters](https://tompeters.com/a-brief-history-of-the-7-s-mckinsey-7-s-model/)
- James C. Collins and Jerry I. Porras, “Building Your Company's Vision” (1996). The article defines vision through core ideology and envisioned future. [Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/1996/09/building-your-companys-vision)

### Academic reviews and later synthesis

- Jeffrey Bracker, “The Historical Development of the Strategic Management Concept” (1980). The review places Drucker and other foundational work in the development of strategic management. [Academy of Management Review](https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/amr.1980.4288731)
- Sebastian Desmidt, Anita Prinzie, and Adelien Decramer, “Looking for the Value of Mission Statements: A Meta-analysis of 20 Years of Research” (2011). The study reports a small positive relationship with performance and significant variation between measures. [Management Decision](https://doi.org/10.1108/00251741111120806)
- Azaddin Salem Khalifa, “Mission, Purpose, and Ambition: Redefining the Mission Statement” (2012). The paper documents the lack of agreement about mission-statement definitions and scope. [Journal of Strategy and Management](https://doi.org/10.1108/17554251211247553)
- Marcel Planellas, *Strategic Decisions*. The book presents mission, vision, and values together as a current strategic-management framework. [Cambridge University Press excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811084/86248/excerpt/9781108486248_excerpt.pdf)
